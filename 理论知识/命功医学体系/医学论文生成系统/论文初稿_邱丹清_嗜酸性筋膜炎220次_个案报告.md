## Abstract

**Background**: Eosinophilic fasciitis (EF) is a rare autoimmune disease characterized by fascial inflammation, sclerosis, and eosinophilic infiltration, with an incidence of approximately 1 in 1,000,000. Standard Western treatment relies on corticosteroids and immunosuppressants, which carry significant side effects and variable efficacy. Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM) external therapies for EF have rarely been documented in modern medical literature.

**Case Presentation**: A 46-year-old female patient presented with left arm swelling, hardening, atrophy, and necrosis following environmental exposure in 2009. She was diagnosed with eosinophilic fasciitis. After conventional treatment showed limited efficacy, she underwent Qimai Medicine therapy (a TCM external meridian conditioning system) from 2015 to 2018 (~200 sessions), followed by a 7-year interruption and resumption in 2025 (220 sessions total).

**Results**: After 200 sessions of Qimai Medicine, the patient demonstrated: (1) functional recovery of the left hand (from severely limited to basically normal activity); (2) tissue regeneration (skin elasticity recovery, pore and hair regrowth on the affected arm); (3) sensory recovery (from complete loss to normal perception); (4) resolution of concurrent "head wind" syndrome (chronic headache requiring constant hat-wearing); and (5) fundamental constitutional improvement (reduced susceptibility to colds and allergies, improved sleep). Currently at 220 sessions (2026), the patient continues to show progressive improvement with deep detoxification responses (deep red sha, blood blister formation) indicating ongoing deep fascial repair.

**Conclusion**: This case suggests that Qimai Medicine's external meridian conditioning—focusing on dispelling wind, cold, and dampness while promoting blood circulation—may achieve functional recovery and tissue regeneration in eosinophilic fasciitis without corticosteroid side effects. The mechanism appears to involve removing pathogenic obstruction (wind-cold-dampness-blood stasis) rather than immunosuppression, thereby restoring blood circulation and activating self-healing. Larger controlled studies are needed to validate these findings.

**Keywords**: Eosinophilic fasciitis; Qimai Medicine; traditional Chinese medicine; meridian conditioning; autoimmune disease; tissue regeneration; case report

## 1. Introduction

### 1.1 Disease Background

Eosinophilic fasciitis (EF), first described by Shulman in 1974, is a rare autoimmune disease characterized by symmetrical fascial inflammation, thickening, and sclerosis, accompanied by peripheral blood eosinophilia and/or tissue eosinophilic infiltration [1,2]. The estimated incidence is approximately 1 in 1,000,000, with a slight female predominance and peak onset between 30-60 years of age [3].

**Clinical features** include:
- Rapid onset of limb swelling, pain, and skin induration
- "Peau d'orange" (orange peel) skin appearance
- Progressive joint contractures and functional limitation
- Potential visceral involvement (rare)

**Diagnostic criteria** require:
- Characteristic skin and fascial changes
- Histopathological confirmation (fascial thickening with eosinophilic infiltration)
- Peripheral eosinophilia (>0.5×10⁹/L or >10% of total white blood cells)
- Elevated inflammatory markers (ESR, CRP)
- MRI showing fascial thickening (helpful but not mandatory) [4]

**Standard Western treatment** consists of:
- Corticosteroids (prednisone 0.5-1 mg/kg/day) as first-line therapy
- Immunosuppressants (methotrexate, cyclophosphamide) for refractory cases
- Physical therapy for contracture prevention

**Prognosis**: Variable. Some patients respond well to steroids, while others show poor response, disease progression, or significant functional disability. Side effects of long-term steroid use (osteoporosis, diabetes, infection risk) remain problematic [5].

### 1.2 Traditional Chinese Medicine Perspective

In Traditional Chinese Medicine (TCM), conditions resembling eosinophilic fasciitis fall under the categories of "Bi Syndrome" (痹证, obstruction syndrome) or "Pi Syndrome" (皮痹, skin obstruction). The *Huangdi Neijing* states: "When wind, cold, and dampness three qi combine, they form Bi syndrome" [6].

**Pathogenesis** (TCM theory):
- External pathogenic factors: Wind, cold, and dampness invade the body
- Internal factors: Qi deficiency, blood stasis, phlegm-dampness accumulation
- Mechanism: Pathogenic factors obstruct meridians → fascia loses nourishment → hardening and atrophy

**Treatment principles**:
- Dispel wind, cold, and dampness
- Promote blood circulation and remove blood stasis
- Nourish fascia and soft tissues
- Tonify Qi and blood

TCM external therapies (acupuncture, moxibustion, massage, cupping) have been reported for various autoimmune conditions, but systematic documentation for eosinophilic fasciitis is extremely limited [7].

### 1.3 Qimai Medicine

Qimai Medicine (炁脉医学) is an integrated traditional Chinese medicine system developed by Dr. Liu Qingyuan, combining TCM meridian theory, modern anatomy, and energy medicine principles [8]. Its core components include:

**Five-Dimensional Diagnosis** (五维诊断): Assessment across five dimensions—Qi (炁), Toxin (毒), Vessel (脉), Pathogen (邪), and Addiction (瘾)—each graded 1-10.

**54-Card Meridian Conditioning** (54牌经络调理): A structured treatment protocol using 54 specific card-based treatment combinations targeting different meridians and pathological patterns.

**Eighteen Transformations** (十八变): A predictive framework for treatment responses and healing crises during long-term therapy.

The theoretical framework proposes that autoimmune diseases like EF result from pathogenic obstruction (wind-cold-dampness-blood stasis) in the meridians and fascia, rather than purely intrinsic immune dysfunction. Treatment aims to remove obstruction, restore circulation, and activate the body's self-healing capacity [8].

### 1.4 Research Gap

Despite the theoretical basis for TCM external therapy in EF, there is a paucity of documented cases in modern medical literature. This case report aims to:
1. Document a rare disease treated with Qimai Medicine external therapy
2. Present objective functional and tissue-level outcomes
3. Propose a mechanism of action based on TCM theory
4. Provide a foundation for future research

## 6. Discussion

### 6.1 Significance of This Case

Eosinophilic fasciitis is an extremely rare autoimmune disease with limited treatment options and variable prognosis. This case represents, to our knowledge, the first documented instance of EF treated with systematic TCM external meridian conditioning over 200 sessions, achieving:

1. **Functional recovery**: From severely limited to basically normal left hand activity
2. **Tissue regeneration**: Skin elasticity recovery, pore and hair regrowth
3. **Sensory recovery**: From complete loss to normal perception
4. **No corticosteroid side effects**: Avoiding osteoporosis, diabetes, and infection risks

These outcomes challenge the conventional understanding that EF requires immunosuppressive therapy and suggest that restoring meridian circulation may be a viable alternative or complementary approach.

### 6.3 Proposed Mechanism

**Western perspective**:
The improvement may be explained by:
- Enhanced local microcirculation through meridian conditioning
- Mechanical stimulation promoting fascial remodeling
- Neuro-immunomodulation through sustained tactile therapy
- Stress reduction and parasympathetic activation

**TCM perspective**:
- Dispelling wind-cold-dampness removes pathogenic obstruction
- Promoting blood circulation restores fascial nourishment
- Tonifying Qi-Blood supports tissue regeneration
- "Where there is free flow, there is no pain; where there is nourishment, there is regeneration"

**Qimai Medicine perspective**:
The theoretical framework proposes that:
1. EF is not primarily an "immune error" but a "circulatory obstruction"
2. Eosinophil accumulation is a repair attempt, not an attack
3. Removing pathogenic obstruction (wind-cold-dampness-blood stasis) allows natural healing
4. Long-term treatment (200+ sessions) is needed for deep fascial remodeling

### 6.4 Limitations

1. **Single case**: Results cannot be generalized; controlled studies needed
2. **Lack of laboratory data**: Peripheral eosinophil counts, ESR, CRP during treatment not systematically recorded (being retrieved)
3. **No imaging follow-up**: MRI of left arm not repeated during treatment
4. **Treatment interruption**: 7-year gap complicates outcome attribution
5. **Placebo effect**: Cannot be ruled out without blinding
6. **Multiple interventions**: Dietary therapy and foot soaks were concurrent

### 6.5 Clinical Implications

This case suggests that:
1. TCM external meridian conditioning may be a viable complementary therapy for EF
2. Long-term treatment (200+ sessions) may be necessary for deep fascial remodeling
3. The mechanism may involve fascial microcirculation improvement rather than immunosuppression
4. The "detoxification response" (sha formation, blood blisters) may serve as a biomarker of treatment depth

### 6.6 Future Research Directions

1. **Case series**: Collect 5-10 EF cases treated with Qimai Medicine
2. **Controlled study**: Randomized controlled trial comparing Qimai Medicine + standard care vs. standard care alone
3. **Mechanism research**: 
   - Before/after MRI of affected limbs
   - Peripheral eosinophil counts and inflammatory markers
   - Fascial biopsy before/after treatment
   - Microcirculation assessment (laser Doppler)
4. **Biomarker development**: Standardize sha assessment and "18 Transformations" as treatment response indicators
